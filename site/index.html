Our rooftop solar, read from the bills

Piyush Dash · Satya Vihar, Bhubaneswar · data to 20 November 2026

In February 2026 a 3 kW solar system went up on our family's roof. It has no app and no data logger, just the electricity bills, the utility's daily meter readings, and free NASA satellite weather. This page shows what that is enough to learn.

Oct bill: the panels made 384.4 kWh against a forecast of 389 published before the bill arrived. The house bought 604.4 kWh, sent 175.2 kWh back, and paid ₹2,414; without solar it would have paid about ₹2,439 more.

November so far (to 20 November): 214 kWh, or 10.7 a day against 12.1 forecast. A cyclone in the Bay of Bengal brought cloud and rain from 18 November, and output fell to 4.7, 1.95 and 1.25 kWh on the 18th, 19th and 20th.

What it has made, and what to expect

Solid bars are what the meter recorded each month; pale bars are the forecast for the next twelve months, with the range ten years of past weather would give. We use kilowatt-hours per day so that short and long billing months compare fairly.

A typical year should give about 4,544 kWh, somewhere between 4,279 and 4,671 depending on the weather. 2026 itself is a poor guide: July and August were 21% and 19% below normal for sunlight, darker than any of the previous ten years.

Sunlight explains almost everything

Plot each month's output per day against the sunlight that reached the ground and the points fall close to a straight line. Sunlight alone explains 93% of the month-to-month change. The sunlight figures come from NASA's POWER service, which estimates them from satellites for the square of map around our house.

That straight line is our first model, a simple regression. The second is a physics model: sunlight on the tilted panels rather than on flat ground, a small loss when the panels run hot, and one efficiency factor fitted to the bills. The two are compared side by side further down.

Checked one day at a time

In November we got the utility's daily chart for the solar meter itself, so we could compare the model with what the panels made each day. We read most values off the portal's chart and took exact figures from its meter table for 13–20 November. The chart readings agreed with the exact ones to within 0.06 kWh a day, and with the meter's total for 1–12 November to within 0.3%.

Over 26 days, 21 Oct to 15 Nov, the physics model's total was within 0.8% of the meter's, and it caught 90% of the day-to-day ups and downs. On clear days it is usually within 6%. The low days are weather, not faults: heavy rain on 27–28 October, cloud on 2 and 4 November, and the cyclone from the 18th.

Two ways to predict, compared

The simple regression uses only the sunlight on flat ground. The physics model also knows the panels are tilted towards the south and lose a little output when hot. To compare them fairly, each month below was predicted by a version of the model fitted to the other months only, so no model ever sees the month it is predicting.

Both models follow the weather equally well from day to day; they differ by season. Held out month by month, the physics model misses less on average, mostly because it handles the hottest, brightest month (May) and August better. It is not better every month: in February and October it guessed high while the regression came closer, February partly because the first day was not a full day of sunshine.

The forecasts differ most in winter. Of the 172 kWh a year between them, 125 come from November to February, because the physics model knows the tilted panels catch more of the low winter sun. November's daily readings side with it so far, but one month is not proof; December and January will be the sharper test. Adding temperature to the regression also helps (a 4.3% typical miss), and adding rain makes it worse.

Where the electricity goes

The house uses solar power directly while the sun is up, sends any surplus to the grid, and buys from the grid at night and on dull days. Under net metering, each unit sent out cancels a unit bought, so the bill is for the difference.

Over the whole period, solar met 26% of the house's needs directly, and the units sent to the grid covered another 20%. Together that is the 46% of the bill paid for by the sun. On the days where we have both meters, the house used 53% of its solar itself, and more of it on sunnier days.

Why the bills went up anyway

The bills rose through spring even though the panels were working well. The reason is the house, not the panels: daily use went from about 19 kWh in February to 35 in June. Each dot below is one day.

Below about 25.5 °C the house uses a steady 18.7 kWh a day. Above it, each degree adds about 2.2 kWh: the air conditioning. The daily data also shows the household's own story. The house was mostly empty from 16 to 23 May, and use fell about 156 kWh below what the heat predicts. Guests from 30 September to 8 October added about 86 kWh.

Is anything wrong with the panels?

We compare each month with what the weather allowed. Only May fell clearly short, at −8.9%: the hottest month, and the longest dry spell. Heat and dust both lower output, and monthly bills cannot tell them apart. A test written down in advance will check this over the winter: if heat was the cause, cool months should come in above expectation.

"Expected" uses one efficiency factor fitted across all months, so the differences compare months with each other. Small differences of a few percent are within the model's normal scatter.

The tilt. We modelled the panels at 12°, facing south. They may be steeper. Testing angles from 5° to 30° against the data, November's daily output fits best at 10–15°; at 20° or more the model would over-forecast winter by 4–7%. Even at 20°, the typical-year forecast moves by only about 1.5%, and May stays the one shortfall.

One way to get more

These are bifacial panels: they also collect light reflected onto their backs. Over a dark concrete roof that is little. Painting the roof under the panels white could add about 509 kWh a year, around 11%, for a few thousand rupees of paint. That is a model estimate, not yet measured on this roof. The extra light also warms the panels slightly, which costs a little of the gain.

What we got wrong along the way

  • The bills print the wrong dates. The daily meter data matched each bill exactly only after shifting the dates. The March bill covered 29 days, not the 31 printed, which had made March look like a bad month. It was not.
  • Satellite data gets revised. NASA later raised its July and August sunlight by 5–9%, which removed what had looked like unexpectedly good monsoon output.
  • Temperature means air, not panels. The heat loss uses NASA's 24-hour average air temperature. Daytime air is warmer, so the heat losses here read slightly low; it changes no conclusion.
  • A printing error on the July bill showed exports doubled (204 kWh instead of 102). The charge itself used the right figure.
