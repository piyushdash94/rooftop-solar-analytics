PART ONE · WHAT HAPPENED

Sun and rain, day by day

Daily sunlight as the filled area, daily rainfall as bars pointing down, monthly generation as dots on the right axis. The monsoon arrives in the second week of June and the array halves.

206 days. The array tracked the sun closely until the rains; from July the sky delivered half the energy it did in May.

Where every unit went

Household demand split into what the panels covered directly and what was bought from the grid. Exported surplus sits separately — it returns as a bill credit.

Solar covered 160–253 kWh a month with remarkable steadiness. The variable was demand: it doubled from February to June as cooling load arrived, then eased with the rains. A 3 kW array against a 1,050 kWh summer month is undersized, not underperforming — the distinction that governs every recommendation below.

PART TWO · WHAT EXPLAINS IT

Sunlight explains almost everything

Average daily irradiance against average daily generation, per billing period.

Correlation +0.96; sunlight alone explains 91% of month-to-month variation. Fitted relationship: generation/day = 2.09 × sunlight + 2.30, residual sd 0.78 kWh/day. Everything else — dust, heat, outages, shading — lives in the remaining 9%. That residual is where the diagnostics are.

Which features predict what

Pearson correlation across seven periods. Bar length is strength, colour is direction.

Cooling degree-days beat raw temperature for predicting import (+0.80 vs +0.43), because import follows air-conditioning runtime rather than heat itself. Cloud fraction is useless at monthly resolution — it averages out. With n = 7 these are descriptive, not inferential.

Two months genuinely under-delivered

March (−9.6%) and May (−10.5%) are the only months that fell short of what the sky offered. Their raw yields differ by 12% — May looks far better — yet their residuals are within a point of each other. That is the entire argument for normalising.

May is the more interesting case: most sunlight of any month, worst conversion, hottest at 39.3 °C average daily high. Heat derating explains part of it; peak pre-monsoon dust plausibly explains the rest. June's residual returns to zero after the first rains washed the panels — a soiling signature.

An earlier pass flagged March as an unexplained anomaly on climatological grounds. Fetching the actual irradiance overturned that: March 2026 received less sun than April. Most of its shortfall was weather. The residual is what survived normalisation. August covers 27 of 31 days; NASA POWER lags about two weeks.

PART THREE · THE LONGER RECORD

The sky is dimming, and it isn't clouds

Annual mean daily irradiance, 1984–2025. Upper line is what a cloud-free sky would have delivered; lower is what arrived.

Measured sunlight fell from 5.14 to 4.65 kWh/m²/day between the first and last decade — −0.15 per decade (p < 0.0001, r² = 0.77).

The diagnostic: clearness index barely moved (0.80 throughout, p = 0.53) while clear-sky irradiance fell faster than measured (−0.18/decade, r² = 0.92). Had clouds been the cause, clearness would have dropped. Instead the cloud-free baseline itself declined — the atmosphere is less transparent on clear days. That is aerosols, and it matches published work attributing dimming over the eastern Indo-Gangetic Plain and eastern coastal regions to rising aerosol loading.

All significant at p < 0.01, units kWh/m²/day. The monsoon was already dim and had less to lose; the clear months carried the decline — precisely the months a rooftop array earns most of its yield.

One number I am flagging rather than reporting: this dataset shows days above 40 °C falling 36% and mean daily maxima declining 0.21 °C per decade. Indian station records show maxima rising, with the literature framing aerosols as partly masking warming rather than reversing it. Outright cooling is more likely a reanalysis artefact than a fact about this city. The irradiance trend is well corroborated; the temperature trend is not, and nothing here is built on it.

Dimming subtracts roughly 10% on top of the 12.5% panels lose to age — about −21% by 2051 rather than the −12.5% a warranty-only calculation gives. Indian rooftop financial models that assume flat irradiance are optimistic by around eight percentage points across an asset life.

The honest limit: aerosols are a policy variable, not a physical constant. Europe and China both reversed their dimming within two decades of tightening air-quality rules. This is the trend-continues case, not a forecast.

Money

What was billed against what the array saved, valued at the slab the avoided units would have fallen into.

Without the array June would have crossed ₹6,300. Across seven months the panels offset about ₹15,500 — roughly ₹2,210 a month.

What to do

  1. Paint the concrete under the array white. These are bifacial modules over dark, often-wet concrete — the datasheet's rear-gain table runs 15–30% depending on ground reflectivity, and the current surface likely yields about 5%. Estimated +12% energy for ₹8–12k, payback near three years. Best value on the list, and it lifts any future panels too.
  2. Photograph the array at 08:30 and 16:30 on a clear day. The water tank and vent pipe sit close to the first row. Two photos settle whether they cast a shadow; nothing in the bills can.
  3. Measure tilt with a phone inclinometer. Tilt is currently an estimate of about 12°, which is the largest single uncertainty in every performance figure here.
  4. Log outage start and end times for one month. Roughly 2–3 hours lost every alternate week, during which a grid-tied inverter shuts down regardless of sunlight. Turns an anecdote into a kWh number.
  5. Check the inverter's DC input rating before expanding. It decides whether 4–4.5 kWp costs ₹47–60k or ₹100k. Expansion above 3 kW gets no further subsidy — PM Surya Ghar caps there.
  6. Skip batteries. Net metering already banks surplus at full retail value. A 5 kWh system costs about ₹5 lakh against ₹3,500–4,000 a year of avoided import.
